Teardown · Architecture

Anatomy of a lead-intelligence pipeline that survives contact with reality

Field note 001 · 2026 · 6 min read

We engineered an end-to-end lead-intelligence platform for a pilot in the US home-services market: a system that monitors public channels for people expressing a service need, qualifies them with an LLM, drafts a locally-branded response, and — only after a human approves — posts it and attributes the result. This note documents the architecture decisions that mattered, because none of them are the ones that make demos impressive.

The shape of the system

CollectionPlatform-native APIs polled on a 15-minute cycle; webhook intake for manual and third-party sources
QualificationLLM extraction of urgency (1–10), hire intent, service type, and location; only urgency ≥ 7 with a confirmed city match passes
ResponseLocally-branded draft, checked against recent replies for wording duplication
Human gateMandatory review dashboard — nothing posts without a person approving it
PostingRate-limited: max 3 posts per run, 30-second delays, per-market daily caps
DedupDatabase-level UNIQUE constraints on replied threads — not application logic
AuditAppend-only post log; every action attributable after the fact

The five decisions that mattered

  1. Dedup lives in the database, not the code. Application-level "have we replied to this?" checks fail under concurrency and redeploys. A UNIQUE constraint cannot forget. If a compliance property matters, enforce it at the layer that cannot be bypassed.
  2. The human gate is a feature, not a compromise. Full autonomy was technically trivial and commercially reckless: one bad automated reply in a community forum costs more than a thousand good ones earn. The approval dashboard is what made the system deployable at all.
  3. Thresholds are conservative by design. Urgency ≥ 7 with city confirmation discards most candidates. In outbound systems, precision is the product; recall is a vanity metric. A missed lead costs nothing visible. A wrong reply is a public artifact.
  4. Rate limits assume the worst day, not the average day. Three posts per run with enforced delays looks timid until the day the classifier has a bad morning — then it is the difference between a quiet incident and a banned account.
  5. Attribution was built before scale. Call-tracking attribution was wired into the design from day one, because "did this make money?" must be answerable from the system of record, not reconstructed from anecdotes later.

The pattern behind all five: production AI engineering is mostly the engineering of what happens when the AI is wrong. The model is the easy 20%. The constraints, gates, limits, and logs are the 80% that make it a business system.

System details are drawn from a platform designed and built by Alra's principal for a home-services pilot engagement. Identifying client details omitted.

Negative result · Evaluation

The AI assistant that confirmed everything and did nothing

Field note 002 · 2026 · 5 min read

This is a note about one of our own systems failing, because publishing only wins is marketing, and publishing losses is engineering. We ran a full audit on a personal AI operating system our principal had built and used daily — a production assistant handling tasks, reminders, and data capture through a chat interface, backed by a relational database and an LLM classification layer.

What the audit found

The assistant confirmed actions it never performed. Asked to schedule reminders, it replied with cheerful confirmations — and the database showed zero reminders had ever been created over the system's entire operating life. Separately, its scheduled backup job had produced 86 backup files, every single one of them 0 bytes. The system reported healthy the whole time.

Nothing was "down." No errors surfaced. Every failure was silent, because the conversational layer — the part the user sees — was decoupled from the execution layer, and nothing measured the gap between them. The assistant was, functionally, a very polite liar: not by intent, but by architecture.

Why this failure mode is the important one

This is not an exotic bug. It is the default failure mode of agentic systems: LLMs generate confirmations from language patterns, not from verified state. Unless the confirmation is generated from the outcome — read back from the database, the calendar, the file that now exists with more than 0 bytes — the system will drift toward confident, fluent, unverified claims. Industry-wide, buyers are discovering this at scale: it is the same mechanism behind fabricated citations in consultancy reports.

The design rules it produced

  1. Confirmations must be read back from state, never generated from intent. "Done" is only sayable after querying the thing that changed.
  2. Every side effect gets an existence check. A backup job that doesn't verify file size > 0 is not a backup job; it is a hope.
  3. Silent success metrics are mandatory. Count actions performed, not actions acknowledged — and alert on the divergence, because divergence is the failure.
  4. Audit your own systems the way you'd audit a vendor's. This failure lived undetected in daily use. Usage is not evaluation.

Every Alra engagement now ships with execution-verification as a non-negotiable: the eval suite counts what the system did, not what it said. This note is why.

Governance brief · Regulated markets

The GCC AI governance stack: PDPL, SDAIA, and the EU AI Act

Field note 003 · 2026 · 8 min read

Boards operating in or with the Gulf now face three overlapping regimes with real dates and real penalties. Most enterprises in the region are adopting AI faster than they are governing it — regional analyses consistently find governance lagging adoption — which makes this the single most consequential compliance gap of 2026. Here is the stack, stripped to what decision-makers need.

1. Saudi PDPL — in force, extraterritorial, and carrying criminal penalties

StatusIn force since 14 Sept 2023; compliance deadline passed 14 Sept 2024. Implementing and Transfer Regulations active.
ReachExtraterritorial — applies to any entity, anywhere, processing data of individuals residing in the Kingdom
PenaltiesUp to SAR 5M for violations; up to SAR 3M and/or 2 years' imprisonment for intentional sensitive-data disclosure; doubled for repeat offences
Operational dutiesRegistration on the National Data Governance Platform for most controllers; DPO for large-scale or sensitive processing; conditions on cross-border transfer

The AI-specific trap: LLM systems ingest, transform, and often transmit personal data across borders by default. A chatbot pipeline calling a US-hosted model with Saudi-resident customer data is a cross-border transfer whether anyone designed it that way or not. PDPL compliance for AI systems is an architecture question, not a policy-document question.

2. SDAIA's AI Adoption Framework — mandatory for government, arriving everywhere else

Issued November 2025, the framework is mandatory for Saudi public-sector entities and rests on five pillars: data governance (inventories and lineage), model accountability (ownership, versioning, audit trails), transparency and explainability for citizen-affecting decisions, human oversight checkpoints for high-risk automation, and AI risk assessment integrated into enterprise risk. In June 2026 SDAIA followed with a package of roughly ten guidance documents — AI ethics, generative-AI principles, deepfake guidelines, professional standards — and a dedicated, risk-classified AI law is under development.

The private-sector transmission mechanism is procurement. Government entities must comply, so their suppliers must demonstrate compliance, so their suppliers must too. Firms that build the governance muscle now sell into that cascade; firms that wait will be disqualified by a due-diligence questionnaire.

3. The EU AI Act — fully applicable, and it reaches the Gulf

The Act became fully applicable on 2 August 2026 (with high-risk obligations under Article 6(1) following by August 2027). Any GCC enterprise serving EU customers, processing EU-resident data, or placing AI-enabled products on the EU market is in scope. Combined with PDPL's extraterritoriality, a Gulf enterprise with European customers now answers to two regimes simultaneously — with different definitions, different risk taxonomies, and different documentation demands.

What a board should actually do

  1. Inventory first. You cannot govern AI systems you have not enumerated — and shadow AI is now the norm, not the exception, inside large organizations.
  2. Assign named accountability. Every regime above converges on the same requirement: a human owner per system, per decision, per dataset.
  3. Make explainability an architecture requirement. Retrofitting audit trails onto a deployed LLM system costs multiples of building them in.
  4. Treat governance as a market advantage, not a tax. In a region where adoption outruns governance by an estimated 9–12 months, being demonstrably compliant is a differentiator competitors cannot fake under audit.

Regulatory facts current as of August 2026; primary sources: Saudi PDPL and Implementing Regulations (SDAIA), SDAIA AI Adoption Framework (Nov 2025), artificialintelligenceact.eu implementation timeline. This brief is general information, not legal advice — engage qualified counsel for compliance decisions.

Method · Evaluation

Define "working" before you build: the eval suite as the real deliverable

Field note 004 · 2026 · 6 min read

Most enterprise AI projects fail at a question nobody wrote down: what does "working" mean? Gartner predicted at least 30% of generative-AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept; S&P Global found 42% of companies abandoned most of their AI initiatives in 2025 — and the leading reason was poor economics despite technical success. The systems ran. Nobody had defined, numerically, what running well meant. So nobody could prove it, defend it, or improve it.

The inversion

At Alra, the evaluation suite is built before the system — and it is the contract's real deliverable. Before a line of pipeline code, we build with the client:

  1. A golden set — 50–150 real cases from the client's own data, each with the agreed-correct outcome. Not synthetic examples: the ugly, ambiguous, real ones, because those are the ones that will arrive in production.
  2. Workflow metrics, in business units — documents correctly processed, leads correctly qualified, cases correctly routed. Never model-side abstractions like perplexity; the CFO cannot take a perplexity number to the board.
  3. Execution verification — counting what the system did, verified from state, not what it said (see Field Note 002 for the audit that made this non-negotiable).
  4. A regression gate — no change ships if the golden-set score drops. Model upgrades, prompt changes, vendor swaps: all pass the same gate. When models change under you — and they do — the gate is what notices.

Why the eval suite outlives everything else

Prompts rot. Models deprecate. Vendors get acquired. The one artifact that survives all of it is the codified definition of correct — because it is expressed in the client's own cases and business units, not in any vendor's terms. It is why our managed-systems retainers include eval maintenance: the suite must evolve as the business does, and a stale definition of "working" is how silent failures return.

This is also the honest foundation for pricing. We quote fixed because the eval suite makes "done" objective: the engagement is complete when the suite says so — not when the hours run out, and not when everyone is too tired to argue.

The discipline behind it

This method comes from systems where correctness is not optional: our principal's background spans institutional valuation practice — where every number must survive an auditor — and production platforms carrying hundreds to over a thousand automated tests under continuous integration. The eval suite is the same discipline, applied to systems whose components happen to be probabilistic. The probabilistic part is precisely why the deterministic harness around it matters more, not less.
